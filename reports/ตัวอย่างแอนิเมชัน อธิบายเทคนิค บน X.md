# Make the clearance check the hero

The main finding is a gap. No creator with real reach on X has animated permission-aware RAG. The few vendor animations that do (AuthZed, Auth0, Microsoft Mechanics) drew between 131 and about 6,600 views, so a Royal Thai Navy film that shows permissions applied *before* search would fill an empty slot rather than copy a format. The examples worth copying fall into three groups. Short educator animations that follow one item through the system supply the motion grammar: Victoria Slocum's 20-second RAG pipeline, Leonie Monigatti's 13-second vector search and Femke Plantinga's keyword-versus-meaning shapes. The most-viewed RAG posts, from Daily Dose of Data Science and ByteByteGo (130k–540k views), are near-static reference diagrams that overwhelm first-time viewers. Vendor access-control clips supply the permission devices: a document grid whose forbidden items fade to grey, a "same question, wrong user" demonstration, and Microsoft's line about tightening access "before a single prompt is sent". Code-rendered explainers made with AI agents (Addy Osmani's browser pipeline, Anthropic's Claude Motion launch, @kimmonismus's history of attention) supply the craft: one persistent hero object, one accent colour with a job, orientation chrome and real reading holds.

For the 90-second Thai film that adds up to a concrete recipe:

- **Spine:** one commander's question and one scanned memo, carried through about eight chapters.
- **Metaphor:** one records-room image in which the clerk checks clearance before entering the stacks.
- **Climax:** a split screen where search-then-filter leaves red residue in logs, result counts and summaries.
- **Text:** capped at Netflix's Thai ceiling of 17 characters per second (plan for about 10), 35 base characters per line and two lines.
- **Type:** a loopless Thai sans with generous line height, no italics, and English terms as small chips.

The note on ASCII motion, After Effects, Higgsfield and motion-agent harnesses (`tooling_ascii_ae_higgsfield_harness.md`) was not in the folder when this was written, so that part is still in progress.

## Retrieval explainers that work follow one item and transform it in place

The clearest pieces follow **one concrete item** and reveal each stage only when that item reaches it. Victoria Slocum's 20-second film types "What is Weaviate?" into a grey chat mock-up. The question becomes a Query chip and runs to a Vector DB tile labelled "(1) Retrieve". A Context pill comes out and fills one of two slots on a Prompt card ("(2) Augment"). An LLM tile follows ("(3) Generate"), and a Response bubble lands back in the same chat. The film spends **about 3–4 seconds per stage**, holds the finished picture for about 5 seconds, ends with about 20 words on screen, and stays strictly greyscale, with black pills only for what the user sees ([Victoria Slocum](https://x.com/victorialslocum/status/1792904826013004076)).

Leonie Monigatti's 13-second piece is the purest motion explainer in the sample. A "Puppy" pill morphs into "[3, 1]", shrinks to a dot and lands on a grid. Cat, Chicken, Cow and Dog fly in so that related animals cluster. A **red "Kitten" query dot** lands next to Cat, a red radius grows around it, and everything outside fades grey. There are at most six one-word labels and no title ([Leonie Monigatti](https://x.com/helloiamleonie/status/1813091293163061620)). That morph (text to numbers to a place on a map) is the best lay transition for embeddings found anywhere. The fade-to-grey is exactly the gesture the Navy film needs for "documents you are not cleared to see".

Contrast is how the good pieces justify hybrid search. Femke Plantinga encodes **method as shape and state as colour**. Keyword search links the query to exactly one grey square. Semantic search links it to a neighbourhood of circles. A "Hybrid Search" bracket then merges a cluster containing both shapes, so the idea lands without any maths. Her diagram does hold unchanged for about 15 seconds while she talks, and a text-only film cannot afford that ([Femke Plantinga](https://x.com/femke_plantinga/status/1811737318710997435)). LlamaIndex runs one question through two failing methods. Semantic-only search leaves the decisive chunk ghosted in a dashed box ("conditions list cut off"). Grep-only floods the screen ("torches latency and token budgets"). The resolution is the three-word card "Agents need both". Each failure card holds about 3.5 seconds with a 9–12-word verdict ([LlamaIndex](https://x.com/llama_index/status/2071656315210826006)).

For an archive, the local equivalent is concrete. Keyword search finds an order number exactly but misses a decades-old memo written in older spelling. Meaning-based search finds the memo but ranks the order low. Hybrid search catches both.

Reranking reads best as sorting or judging. Cohere shows a jumbled pile of candidate tiles reordered into a ranked list ([Cohere](https://x.com/cohere/status/1815780869384069524)). The Jev diagram draws a **scoreboard with a cut line**: candidates above the threshold survive and the rest are struck through. It then shows a grounded answer that "cites C7 · C2 · C15" and an explicit abstain path, reply "not in the docs" ([Akshay Pachaar](https://x.com/akshay_pachaar/status/2105281570898849903)). Citations plus a visible "not found" branch are the two devices most likely to earn a commander's trust. Copy those devices without the density.

Three examples matter specifically for a 120-year multimodal archive:

- **Search over page images.** Jo Kristian Bergum's demo shows query words as chips. Clicking "Switzerland" lays a heat map over a PDF page *image*, revealing which patches of a chart matched. In under three seconds it proves the system searched the picture, not just the text ([Jo Kristian Bergum](https://x.com/jobergum/status/1858858710065369262)).
- **Parsing.** Jerry Liu's parsing demo links a highlighted PDF region to its extracted text with an orange highlight ([Jerry Liu](https://x.com/jerryjliu0/status/2067679507126124858)).
- **Chunking.** Weaviate colour-bands one real Wikipedia page (Loch Ness) three ways: by tokens, sentences and sections ([Weaviate](https://x.com/weaviate_io/status/1960697892466765829)). The late-chunking video highlights "Its more than 3.85 million inhabitants" to show a chunk that has lost its subject, which explains in one beat why every chunk needs context and metadata ([Femke Plantinga](https://x.com/femke_plantinga/status/1953108821360165032)).

Metadata riding along with each chunk appears only in static form, in two places. Akshay Pachaar's 2024 diagram draws the vector database as a table with a **Metadata column beside Text Chunk** ([Akshay Pachaar](https://x.com/akshay_pachaar/status/1748322389321265261)). Aurimas Griciūnas draws a Metadata Index next to the Vector Index ([Aurimas Griciūnas](https://x.com/Aurimas_Gr/status/1858862365824974909)).

No X animation found shows OCR of a degraded historical scan, table extraction, or chart-to-text conversion. None places permission filtering relative to hybrid search and reranking. The Navy film has to invent those beats. One habit not to import is Weaviate's one-word karaoke captions. Thai has no spaces between words, so word-by-word segmentation would read awkwardly.

## Permission-aware RAG is a vendor niche with three- and four-digit view counts

The few animations of the actual subject come from security vendors, and each teaches one device.

- **AuthZed** (131 views) shows a 6-second loop of its interactive demo. A user picker set to "Bob (Eng)" asks about "Q4 salaries". A trace types out "No permission checks. All retrieved docs sent to LLM", the AI answers with salary bands, and a four-box pipeline ends in a red-glowing **"Data Leak"** box ([AuthZed](https://x.com/authzed/status/2072679204831768894)). The demo's own fix is the opposite of the Navy's message: it is titled "Retrieve first, check permissions second" and post-filters per document. Its final step is worth borrowing, though: "Bob can't view salary data, so his AI assistant can't either" ([AuthZed demo](https://authzed.com/ai-authorization/rag-demo)).
- **Auth0's** 19-second 16:9 clip (497 views) slides a glowing agent token along one thin line into a shielded panel. The panel opens into a grid of about 15 documents, and **the forbidden ones gain red ✕ badges and turn grey**. The whole clip uses about 12 caption words, with English terms in uppercase monospace pills ([Auth0](https://x.com/auth0/status/2026316145792262237)).
- **Microsoft Mechanics** is the only source that animates label inheritance. Its captions read "the label is also automatically inherited by anything the agent generates from it" while a label banner appears on the AI-written document. The script's key phrase, "tighten access at the source **before a single prompt is sent**", is the closest wording to the Navy's message found anywhere ([Microsoft Mechanics](https://x.com/MSFTMechanics/status/2072852381981655283)). A companion short lets an agent summarise purchase orders and then stops it at a labelled contract. A magenta band sweeps behind the refusal, which shows the rule firing per item rather than per session ([Microsoft Mechanics](https://x.com/MSFTMechanics/status/2106811439994302969)).

The clearest picture of filter order is still static. Leonie Monigatti's two-row figure runs the same seven black chips through "Filter → Vector search" and through "Vector search → Filter". In the second row, **only one of three top results survives**, and the caption warns of a "risk of incomplete or zero results" ([Leonie Monigatti](https://x.com/helloiamleonie/status/1849103678185037984)). Level Up Coding frames the same split as "What works in demos vs what works at scale" ([Nikki Siapno](https://x.com/NikkiSiapno/status/2012515066856493365)). Akshay Pachaar counts "500 similarity calculations to surface 12 results" ([Akshay Pachaar](https://x.com/akshay_pachaar/status/2038520658817397000)). Pinecone puts it as "We wanted ten results, and we've returned four" ([Pinecone](https://www.pinecone.io/learn/vector-search-filtering/)).

All of these argue recall and speed. The Navy film argues confidentiality, which is a different case. Articles state it: by the time a late filter runs, confidential content "has already left the store and entered the pipeline" ([Tarento](https://www.tarento.com/articles/permission-aware-rag-enterprise-ai-security/)), and unauthorised chunks "can still affect ranking, traces, or intermediate caches when filtering happens late" ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/imversion_tech/secure-enterprise-rag-enforcing-user-level-access-control-best-practi-1jf7)). These two came from search summaries; the pages were not opened. **No X post visualises leakage into logs, result counts, caches or summaries.** Showing that leak as residue would be the film's original contribution.

The metaphors in use on X are mechanical: grey-out, ✕ and ✓ badges, shields, and keys. Level Up Coding's SSO GIF calls SSO "a master key to open all different locks" and moves one orange dot through numbered steps ([Nikki Siapno](https://x.com/NikkiSiapno/status/1798343266766819396)). Its SQL-order GIF (172,003 views) enlarges each step in turn so that order becomes the subject ([Nikki Siapno](https://x.com/NikkiSiapno/status/1721521603387801618)). Auth0 says an agent "shouldn't have admin keys and crossed fingers", but that post's 143,635 views are out of line with the account's usual 500–900 and were probably promoted ([Auth0](https://x.com/auth0/status/2057508914959380887)). Avi Chawla's agent diagram tags its orchestrator "2 tools only · never searches", a compact way to show tools scoped per agent ([Avi Chawla](https://x.com/_avichawla/status/2058837753954238510)). The library "restricted section" with a librarian as the authorisation layer appears only off X, in a MongoDB/Permit.io tutorial ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/mongodb/guarding-the-restricted-section-a-magical-approach-to-secure-rag-with-mongodb-permitio-and-1dp0)). The highest-reach access-control posts on X, such as ByteByteGo's authentication-mechanisms chart at 378,343 views, are static images ([ByteByteGo](https://x.com/bytebytego/status/1708362516068458748)).

The best executive framing is Microsoft's. "Copilot only surfaces what a user can already open, exposing years of oversharing… Your **permissions debt** goes live the day Copilot does. Fix it first" ([Microsoft Mechanics](https://x.com/MSFTMechanics/status/2082531103433265477)). Its oversharing short closes calmly, on a policy run "in simulation mode" before enforcement ([Microsoft Mechanics](https://x.com/MSFTMechanics/status/2108985767804502111)). That honesty matters for the Navy. Pre-filtering guarantees the AI sees no more than the access lists allow, and Microsoft's own documentation makes the same one-line promise: Copilot "only surfaces organizational data to which individual users have at least view permissions" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/microsoft-365-copilot/microsoft-365-copilot-privacy)). If those access lists are wrong, the AI makes the mistake instant and searchable.

## Agent-rendered explainers win on continuity and restraint

The strongest code-rendered technical explainers share three traits: a **persistent protagonist object**, restrained art direction, and on-screen orientation devices.

- **Addy Osmani's** 40-second browser film alternates a warm cream "storybook" world with a navy "blueprint" diagram world. A red mascot carries the request through DNS, TLS, parsing, layout, paint and compositing. A lowercase scene label sits top-left and a step dial ("1 · fetch" to "6 · composite") top-right. A persistent "hello, web" page is carried through the final stages ([Addy Osmani](https://x.com/addyosmani/status/2103009037164110327)). Its cuts land **every 2.0 seconds for the first 20 seconds**, 17 cuts in all. That is about one concept per 2.3 seconds: fine for developers enjoying the craft, far too fast for commanders reading Thai.
- **Anthropic's Claude Motion launch** (81.6 s, one hard cut) shows the brief that matters for executive work: "keep it simple: the hill running dry by 8:15, and the 92 bikes we carry back up". It colours only the two entities in the story and greys everything else, and shows an edit given in timing language, "Hold on the empty dock a beat longer so it lands" ([Anthropic](https://x.com/claudeai/status/2108271552991252810)). Anthropic says Claude writes each animation as code, "with no video model involved" ([Anthropic](https://x.com/claudeai/status/2108271558170976743)).
- **@kimmonismus's** three-minute history was made in Claude Code as about **7,400 lines of Remotion** in about an hour. One glowing token sits near the centre for 180 seconds with zero hard cuts, and each beat gets one serif caption line with one coloured keyword ([@kimmonismus](https://x.com/kimmonismus/status/2102844654169575547)).
- **Alisa Qian's** fat-loss explainer models a story spine the Navy film can reuse. It runs from myth to question, mechanism, maths with the source citation on screen, the misconception killed, a recap, and a callback to the hook, oriented by numbered chapter chips in a three-ink risograph palette ([Alisa Qian](https://x.com/alisaqqt/status/2102749340527919471)).
- **Misbah Syed's** HyperFrames pipeline draws "three layers of defense" as numbered shields, which maps directly onto permission sync at ingest, filtering at retrieval, and citation at answer time ([Misbah Syed](https://x.com/MisbahSy/status/2045159435505660161)).
- **Michael Guo's** GPU isolation explainer colour-codes two tenants cyan and orange, a good device for two officers with different clearances. It ends as a poster of phone-unreadable text, the end state to avoid ([Michael Guo](https://x.com/Michaelzsguo/status/2052354444331815386)).
- **A GA4 data story** headlines its conclusion, shows evidence, closes on a "SO WHAT" card with a "Next" action, and labels its numbers "dummy data" on screen ([Himanshu Sharma](https://x.com/analyticsnerd/status/2108528768079876510)).

The open-source motion skills turn this craft into numbers. whaleyxbt's guide prefers persistent objects over cuts and allows "one hero moment per video". It sets entrances on `bezier(0.16, 1, 0.3, 1)` at 0.7–1.25 s, with exits at about half that. Its reading holds are **at least 1 s plus about 0.25 s per word beyond three, and at least 2 s for the final hook**. It also notes that "the 22 s cut of the effort video exists because the 15 s one flashed text too fast" ([whaleyxbt motion-design skill](https://github.com/whaleyxbt/claude-motion/blob/main/.claude/skills/motion-design/SKILL.md)). Its review loop checks that frame 0 is not black, because X autoplays muted and the first frame is the thumbnail ([whaleyxbt review-loop skill](https://github.com/whaleyxbt/claude-motion/blob/main/.claude/skills/review-loop/SKILL.md)).

HeyGen's HyperFrames rules ([motion-principles](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes/blob/main/skills/hyperframes-creative/references/motion-principles.md)) add:

- the slowest scene should be **three times slower than the fastest**;
- scenes follow Build (0–30%), Breathe (30–70%) and Resolve (70–100%);
- a full stagger sequence stays under 500 ms;
- hero text fills 60–80% of the frame width;
- layouts anchor to edges rather than floating in the centre;
- pure black reads as "nothing loaded".

For data, "only the VALUE changes", and pie charts, multi-axis charts and six-panel dashboards are banned ([data-in-motion](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes/blob/main/skills/hyperframes-creative/references/data-in-motion.md)). HeyGen also found that a callout reading "122,341" looked random until it was relabelled "PEAK DAY · JUN 21", which makes the label carry the why ([HeyGen](https://x.com/HeyGen/status/2076381187640324427)). The guides disagree in places: whaleyxbt bans more than one focal point per frame, while HyperFrames requires at least two. Choose one rule deliberately rather than inherit both.

The process evidence is consistent:

1. **Brief.** Describe the mechanism, name every state, give the agent the real material, and ask for a plan before code ([N01ennn](https://x.com/N01ennn/status/2107826459804840072)).
2. **Storyboard.** Write it as a table with a "why" column, and cut any frame whose why does not trace back to the message ([HyperFrames story-spine](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes/blob/main/skills/hyperframes-creative/references/story-spine.md)).
3. **Stills before motion.** Approve one still frame per scene before anything moves. Rexan Wong's workflow post (18,205 bookmarks) reports the first render is then "usually 80% there" ([Rexan Wong](https://x.com/rexan_wong/status/2103707054108299437)).
4. **Deterministic frames.** Render every frame as a pure function of time, so a fix is a one-line edit and a re-render ([N01ennn](https://x.com/N01ennn/status/2107826459804840072)).
5. **Checks on every pass.** Run automated checks for overlapping text, text off the stage and fallback fonts. Review contact sheets with a separate checker, because "don't let the agent that did the work decide the work is good" ([N01ennn](https://x.com/N01ennn/status/2107826459804840072)).

"One-shot" claims hide real cost. One viral piece behind a "Claude one-shot this" post rested on a prompt of about 10,000 characters ([Thariq](https://x.com/trq212/status/2102870353781641416)). An honest bill for a 45-second short was **163 model calls, about $34 and about 6¾ hours** ([Mable Joseph](https://x.com/mablesjoseph/status/2103465246014746943)). A teardown found Opus hand-rolling a zero-dependency single-file renderer instead of using Remotion or HyperFrames ([Tommy Rossi](https://x.com/__morse/status/2103485566570369333)). So the Navy brief should name its engine explicitly and budget tens of iterations.

## A 90-second spine: one question, one memo, one boundary drawn before search

Research backs the structure the best X pieces converge on:

- **Narrative.** Narrative formats give "increased comprehension, interest, and engagement" over logical-scientific ones ([Dahlstrom, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4183170)).
- **Mayer's multimedia principles** favour segmenting (d = 0.70), signalling (d = 0.46), temporal contiguity (d = 1.30) and spatial contiguity (d = 0.79) ([Mayer principles review](https://www.synapsesocial.com/es/papers/69dbbbdacebd566818835a69)). Other versions of the review report different values.
- **No voice-over.** The same review gives the **modality advantage of narration (d = 0.72)**, which this film gives up. So text must be minimal and set next to the object it names.
- **Length.** Engagement holds steady to about two minutes across 564,710 videos ([Wistia](https://wistia.com/blog/optimal-video-length)).

Four story shapes recur in high-reach lay explainers. Anthropic and Linear follow one item through a process ([Anthropic NLA](https://x.com/AnthropicAI/status/2052435436157452769); [Linear](https://x.com/linear/status/2071983086708150721)). Anthropic's hardware-standard film turns a tangle of point-to-point links into one hub, a before/after ([Anthropic](https://x.com/AnthropicAI/status/2093038426140651791)). Its Fermat proof grows a structure while counters rise, with zero cuts ([Anthropic](https://x.com/AnthropicAI/status/2095947707605266436)). IBM builds pillars cumulatively ([IBM](https://x.com/IBM/status/2102397505472192815)). The Navy film should use the first as its spine, the scale counter for the archive, and the before/after as its climax. A persistent timeline ruler like 3Blue1Brown's IMO trailer ([3Blue1Brown](https://x.com/3blue1brown/status/2101009650686492990)), dated in พ.ศ. (Buddhist Era), carries the 120 years.

Executive framing should be answer-first. BLUF (bottom line up front) comes from the US Army's own writing standard ([BLUF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BLUF_(communication))). An experiment with 650 people found **jargon raised risk perception and lowered support for adopting a technology** ([Bullock et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31354058/)). A follow-up found defining the terms "made no difference" ([Shulman et al. via ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/02/200212084357.htm)). So each screen leads with plain Thai, and the English term rides along as a small tag at its first use, in line with GOV.UK's rule that technical terms are fine if explained the first time ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk)). IBM's habit of turning each layer into a leader's control question ("Who can access it?") and Google Thailand's control-first ordering, "ข้อมูลของคุณ คุณควบคุมได้" ("your data, you control it"), both point the same way: put security before speed for an audience whose main fear is classified leakage ([IBM](https://x.com/IBM/status/2102397505472192815); [Google Thailand](https://x.com/GoogleThailand/status/2044687236394881471)).

Two cautions follow. Do not invent figures: Microsoft's "more than 30,000 files at risk" came from its own demo, and illustrative numbers should be labelled as examples on screen ([Microsoft Mechanics](https://x.com/MSFTMechanics/status/2108985767804502111)). And do not promise perfect security. Promise that the AI follows the same clearance rules as the files.

Use one controlling metaphor, and make the embedding map part of it rather than a second image. NVIDIA's canonical lay analogy casts RAG as a court clerk sent to the law library for precedents the judge can cite "like footnotes" ([NVIDIA](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/what-is-retrieval-augmented-generation/)). Extended for a navy, the clerk works a **records room whose floor plan is the embedding map**:

- related files are shelved near one another (Leonie's neighbourhood);
- the clerk's lamp is the search circle;
- rooms above the officer's clearance are locked and dimmed *before* the clerk walks in, which is pre-filtering;
- the careless clerk instead carts every relevant file to the desk and then tries to hide some, which is post-filtering.

Each archive item carries a classification stamp and an access-list tag. When the item is split into chunks, the stamp duplicates onto every fragment, and the tags point back to one access-list registry ledger that decides. This mirrors AuthZed's "doc ID → permission registry" pattern ([AuthZed demo](https://authzed.com/ai-authorization/rag-demo)). The AI assistant borrows the officer's badge rather than holding a master key. The answer inherits the highest label of its sources, as in Microsoft's banner. Metaphors can mislead ([Taylor & Dewsbury](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969428)), so add one beat that marks where it stops: unlike a clerk, the AI can be wrong, so it always shows its pages. "Blast radius" is military-friendly vocabulary, but it implies thinking after the incident. A search boundary (ขอบเขตการค้น) drawn before the search is the better anchor.

The recommended beat sheet below synthesises the measured examples. The timings are the research team's recommendation, not a tested standard.

The four research sweeps proposed different beat counts, from six to eight beats of 10–14 s up to fifteen to twenty beats of 4–6 s. They agree once **chapters** are separated from **changes**. Use about eight chapters of 10–12 seconds each, in line with Linear's roughly 13 seconds per stage and IBM's 6–9 seconds per pillar ([Linear](https://x.com/linear/status/2071983086708150721); [IBM](https://x.com/IBM/status/2102397505472192815)). Inside each chapter, make one visible change every 2–4 seconds, as Leonie and Victoria do, for roughly 15–20 text events in total.

Keep the primary English chips to four (RAG, ACL, Pre-filter, Citation), the number the lay-audience sweep recommends. Put component names such as OCR, Hybrid search and Rerank in small monospace labels inside a navy "blueprint" register, alternating with the paper records-room world as Addy Osmani alternates his two worlds. Engineers then get the mechanism without the jargon leading the story. Since there is no narration, the X post copy should carry the detail, as Anthropic's Fermat post does ([Anthropic](https://x.com/AnthropicAI/status/2095947707605266436)).

## Thai text: budget characters, break at phrases, emphasise by weight

### Count base characters, not words

Thai has no spaces between words, so "words per screen" is ill-defined. Netflix counts characters instead. Its Thai guide allows **35 characters per line, not counting tone marks or above/below vowels, a reading speed of at most 17 characters per second for adults, at most two lines with one preferred, and no italics** ([Netflix Thai guide](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220448308)). Each text event lasts between 5/6 of a second and 7 seconds ([Netflix general requirements](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215758617)). The BBC adds that labels deserve "an extra second" and that busy graphics, unfamiliar words and long figures all need more time ([BBC Subtitle Guidelines](https://www.bbc.co.uk/accessibility/forproducts/guides/subtitles/)). Every one of those conditions applies to this film.

Measured Thai examples on X show the range. Google Thailand's Songkran anti-scam steps hold 24–39 base characters for 4.2–6.4 seconds, **about 3.7–8.1 characters per second**, which reads comfortably ([Google Thailand](https://x.com/GoogleThailand/status/1911253005069959256)). Its Deep Research headline runs at about 17, right at the ceiling ([Google Thailand](https://x.com/GoogleThailand/status/1915586560583442889)). Its Personal Intelligence "control" card shows 140 base characters in 3.57 seconds, **about 39 characters per second**, on top of overlapping UI text, and cannot be read ([Google Thailand](https://x.com/GoogleThailand/status/2044687236394881471)). Sound-off design is the norm in any case: 69% of US respondents in a 2019 survey watch video with the sound off in public ([3Play Media](https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/verizon-media-and-publicis-media-find-viewers-want-captions/)).

| Parameter | Ceiling from standards | Plan for the Navy film |
|---|---|---|
| Reading speed | 17 characters/s for adults ([Netflix Thai](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220448308)) | 8–12 characters/s on screens that carry a diagram |
| Line length | 35 base characters ([Netflix Thai](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220448308)) | 35 or fewer; one line wherever possible |
| Lines | 2 maximum, 1 preferred | 1; 2 only on the opening and payoff cards |
| Event duration | 5/6 s to 7 s ([Netflix](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215758617)) | About 3–4 s for one line, 6–7 s for two |
| New term or label | About +1 s ([BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/accessibility/forproducts/guides/subtitles/)) | +1 s whenever an English chip first appears |
| Final card | At least 2 s ([whaleyxbt](https://github.com/whaleyxbt/claude-motion/blob/main/.claude/skills/motion-design/SKILL.md)) | At least 3 s |
| Total on-screen Thai | — | About 600 base characters across about 60 s of text-bearing screen time |
| Numbers | Thai words for 1–10, numerals for 11+, short round forms ([Netflix Thai](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/220448308)) | One number per screen, counted up, with Arabic numerals for headline figures |

### Loopless for headlines, looped for sentences, Sarabun for documents

Thai typeface choice has real evidence behind it. In Typotheque's 2024 Bangkok study of 180 native readers, **looped Thai read slightly faster in paragraphs and was perceived as more readable, and older readers discerned looped forms better**, while loopless type read as "modern" ([Typotheque](https://www.typotheque.com/research/effects-of-loops-in-thai)). Google's own descriptions call Noto Sans Thai "mainly suitable for headlines" and its Looped sibling "suitable for all texts" ([Noto Sans Thai](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/fonts/main/ofl/notosansthai/DESCRIPTION.en_us.html); [Noto Sans Thai Looped](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/fonts/main/ofl/notosansthailooped/DESCRIPTION.en_us.html)).

That points to three roles. A loopless sans sets the headlines and chapter cards. IBM Plex Sans Thai ([Google Fonts](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/fonts/main/ofl/ibmplexsansthai/DESCRIPTION.en_us.html)) and Anuphan, a loopless Thai drawn from IBM Plex ([Google Fonts](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/fonts/main/ofl/anuphan/DESCRIPTION.en_us.html)), both have a matching Latin and Mono for the English chips. A looped face sets any full-sentence line meant for senior officers. Sarabun ("TH Sarabun New", used in the Government Gazette) is kept for recreated archival memos where an official-paper feel is the point ([Google Fonts: Sarabun](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/fonts/main/ofl/sarabun/DESCRIPTION.en_us.html)).

Two cautions apply. Prompt and Mitr warn that look-alike letters (ก ถ ภ, บ ป, ข ช) "might lead to confusion when typesetting very short texts", which is exactly what chips and labels are ([Google Fonts: Prompt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/fonts/main/ofl/prompt/DESCRIPTION.en_us.html)). And a 2010 cabinet resolution designated 13 national fonts that government agencies must use, TH Sarabun PSK foremost ([Wikipedia: National Fonts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Fonts)). Whether that rule binds Navy video graphics is unverified, so check with the communications office before locking the font.

Line setting needs more room than Latin habits allow. Thai marks can surround a base consonant on up to three sides, and spaces separate phrases rather than words ([W3C Thai Layout Requirements](https://www.w3.org/TR/thai-lreq/)). An informal rendering test found **clipping at line-height 1.1 and none at 1.6 or above** ([SuttaCentral forum](https://discourse.suttacentral.net/t/testing-for-line-height-with-tall-scripts/6196)). Set multi-line Thai at about 1.5–1.7, use no negative tracking, and pad any mask reveal so stacked marks are not cut off. Test strings such as "ปู่", "ที่" and "ฤทธิ์" in the chosen font.

Emphasis should come from weight and colour, never italics. Google Thailand bolds "**ขั้นตอน**" inside a heading and puts the key phrase in red ([Google Thailand](https://x.com/GoogleThailand/status/1911253005069959256)). In the Deep Research and THE STANDARD clips, Latin capitals render visibly taller than Thai consonants ([Google Thailand](https://x.com/GoogleThailand/status/1915586560583442889); [THE STANDARD](https://x.com/thestandardth/status/2107797267549761627)), so optically reduce English terms or set them in small monospace chips. Google Thailand's "VC (นักลงทุน)" glosses an English term in Thai. Invert it for lay viewers: plain Thai first, the English term second.

A Chinese HyperFrames explainer with 641,258 views pairs small black English chips ("1970s | C Language") with large native-language headlines, a ready template for Thai ([@BTCqzy1](https://x.com/BTCqzy1/status/2051866228646691321)). A Japanese Claude Motion test shows the failure to avoid: the katakana word for "dashboard" is split mid-word across lines. The same user reports that **Japanese Google Fonts could not be used in Claude Motion** ([@ryu_f_web](https://x.com/ryu_f_web/status/2108504471693385895)).

For Thai, that means:

- set line breaks by hand, at phrase spaces only;
- animate by phrase, line or grapheme cluster, never per character, so tone marks never detach from their consonants;
- ship the font file locally;
- test early whichever engine is used.

HyperFrames' linter already fails any named font without an in-file `@font-face` pointing to a local file ([HyperFrames core skill](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes/blob/main/skills/hyperframes-core/SKILL.md)). Silent font fallbacks are a known bug class ([N01ennn](https://x.com/N01ennn/status/2107826459804840072)). No Thai code-rendered explainer was found on X, so Thai rendering in Remotion, HyperFrames or Claude Motion is untested in public. Render a font and line-break test card before building scenes.

### Layout and colour: a greyscale world, two accents, red only for the leak

The colour discipline that works in motion is consistent across the best pieces. Victoria Slocum stays greyscale, Leonie Monigatti uses one red accent for the query, Auth0 greys out forbidden documents, and Anthropic colours only two entities ([Victoria Slocum](https://x.com/victorialslocum/status/1792904826013004076); [Leonie Monigatti](https://x.com/helloiamleonie/status/1813091293163061620); [Auth0](https://x.com/auth0/status/2026316145792262237); [Anthropic](https://x.com/claudeai/status/2108271552991252810)). As one motion guide puts it, "If everything is coral, nothing is" ([whaleyxbt](https://github.com/whaleyxbt/claude-motion/blob/main/.claude/skills/motion-design/SKILL.md)).

For the Navy film:

- **Components** are greyscale on paper, with navy for the blueprint register.
- **Accent 1** marks the question and its answer.
- **Accent 2** marks the moment the permission check fires, like Microsoft's magenta band ([Microsoft Mechanics](https://x.com/MSFTMechanics/status/2106811439994302969)).
- **Grey with a padlock** marks out-of-scope items.
- **Red** appears only in the post-filter lane and its residue.
- **Classification stamps** get their own hue scale, so they are never confused with allowed or denied states.

Layout should be zone-based, anchored to the frame edges, with persistent orientation chrome: a scene label top-left, a numbered step counter top-right and the พ.ศ. ruler along the bottom, after Addy Osmani and IterIntellectus ([Addy Osmani](https://x.com/addyosmani/status/2103009037164110327); [IterIntellectus](https://x.com/IterIntellectus/status/2103212539895017864)). Never leave the whole pipeline on screen as a poster. Collapse each finished stage into a summary chip, and keep the all-at-once twin-column diagram for the final recap, the one moment when every element is already familiar. Check every frame at 360 px wide ([whaleyxbt](https://github.com/whaleyxbt/claude-motion/blob/main/.claude/skills/motion-design/SKILL.md)). Skip decorative particles and glows, which add load without meaning; one viral UI template bans them outright ([twoclipping](https://x.com/twoclipping/status/2103273003555402193)).

## Conclusion

The evidence reverses the usual way of building this kind of film. The pipeline diagram (OCR → embeddings → vector database → LLM) is the part X has already over-produced as static reference art. The permission boundary is the part nobody has animated for lay viewers, and it is the part a navy audience most needs to trust. A film that spends its climax on the boundary being drawn *before* the lamp is lit, and on the residue post-filtering leaves behind, is both more original and more persuasive than another tidy architecture loop. The residue matters most because the X examples, and most vendor copy, argue filter order on speed and recall. The Navy's case is confidentiality, so the film has to show that a late filter cannot un-see what the AI already touched.

Two practical implications follow. X engagement cannot validate this film: the relevant vendor posts have hundreds of views, and Thai explainers rarely pass a few thousand. The real test is the lay-audience sweep's muted retell. Show the film silently to three non-technical staff and cut text until they can repeat "ถามได้ ตอบเร็ว เห็นเฉพาะที่มีสิทธิ์ มีที่มาทุกคำตอบ" (ask anything, fast answers, see only what you're cleared for, every answer sourced). The riskiest untested element is Thai type in code-rendered engines, not the story, so a test card for fonts and line breaks belongs at the start of production, not the end. The film's honest final ask should also be organisational: pre-filtering only enforces the access lists that exist, so the "permissions debt" has to be paid down before the archive goes live.
